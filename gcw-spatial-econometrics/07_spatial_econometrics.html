Spatial econometric analysis

Research questions, data, model sequence, and results

From the paper to the analysis

The paper builds national forest and marine carbon-wealth accounts and relates them to forest-capacity losses and national economic and emissions indicators. This notebook makes the spatial-econometric part of that workflow inspectable: it shows which country and territory observations enter each relationship, how the variables are transformed, what OLS diagnostics are run, how spatial candidates are compared, and what the fitted models report.

The analysis is organised around three questions already encoded in the project outputs. The first asks how the three forest-account indicators vary with national characteristics. The second asks whether adding 2024 emissions changes those relationships. The third asks how 2024 emissions vary with each forest-account indicator. Each question contains three related specifications, giving nine primary relationships in total.

The word relationship is deliberate. These are cross-sectional associations. The analysis does not identify a causal effect of GCW/GDP, forest-capacity loss, or erosion on emissions.

Research questions

  1. How are forest-capacity loss, relative GCW erosion, and GCW as a share of GDP associated with the baseline national controls?
  2. How do those three relationships change when 2024 emissions are added as a predictor?
  3. How are 2024 CO₂ emissions associated with forest-capacity loss, relative GCW erosion, and GCW/GDP, conditional on the baseline controls?

For example, the third question includes the relationship between GCW/GDP and emissions. It is not phrased as “the impact” because the design is observational and does not establish causal identification.

What each model uses

The outcomes are (1) annual forest-capacity loss in MtCO₂/year, (2) reported annual loss as a percentage of GCW, and (3) GCW as a percentage of 2024 GDP. In the emissions-as-outcome models, the dependent variable is 2024 CO₂ emissions in MtCO₂/year. The 2024 emissions series comes from the current GCB 2025-derived country table.

The common baseline controls are log GDP per capita, log forest area in million hectares, and the share of forest area in warm biomes. For forest-capacity loss and erosion, only units reporting deforestation enter the sample; unreported loss is missing, not zero. Every model uses log1p for its dependent variable. GDP per capita and forest area use natural-log transforms; the added emissions predictor uses log1p of MtCO₂/year. When a forest-account indicator is added to an emissions-as-outcome model, that predictor is also log1p transformed.

The two neighbourhood definitions are row-standardised Queen contiguity and row-standardised symmetrised k-nearest neighbours with k = 5, constructed from representative points in an equal-area projection. Queen isolates are retained with no neighbours; kNN can connect territories across water. The next section maps what those non-zero relationships look like.

2. Spatial neighbourhoods and weight matrices

For a given analysis, each row and column of a spatial-weights matrix refers to one country or territory in that model’s sample. A non-zero entry means that two units are defined as neighbours; zero means they are not direct neighbours. Before fitting, the full neighbour graph is restricted to the complete-case sample for that outcome and the remaining rows are standardised. Each non-isolated row therefore sums to 1; an isolated Queen row sums to 0.

The maps below use one concrete example: the 120-unit complete-case sample for the baseline annual forest-capacity-loss outcome. They are not a claim that all nine relationships use the same sample. Queen links join polygons that share an edge or a corner. The kNN-5 graph starts with each unit’s five nearest representative points in EPSG:6933, then symmetrises the links: if either unit selects the other, the pair is connected. After symmetrising, a unit can have more than five neighbours.

Both matrices start with a binary link: a neighbour has weight 1 and a non-neighbour has weight 0. Row standardisation divides each row’s non-zero weights by that unit’s neighbour count. So, for example, if a unit has four neighbours, each receives weight 1/4 and the row sums to 1. An isolate has no links and its row sums to 0. The map fill shows neighbour count; line width shows the average of the two directed row-standardised weights for that pair.

The fill shows each unit’s neighbour count. A line shows a non-zero neighbour relation; line width is the average of the two directed row-standardised weights for display. The maps are built by calling the same Python geometry and weights functions used by the estimation script. Rendering this section reconstructs the graph but does not fit models. Links crossing the map’s antimeridian seam are omitted from the drawn lines only; they remain in the matrices and models.

4. Research question 1: forest-account indicators and baseline controls

Question: How are annual capacity loss, relative GCW erosion, and GCW/GDP associated with GDP per capita, forest area, and warm-biome share?

The sequence begins with OLS using heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors as a non-spatial reference. Moran’s I then describes spatial autocorrelation in the outcome and OLS residuals. Lagrange-multiplier tests evaluate specified forms of spatial dependence relative to OLS. These diagnostics do not automatically choose a spatial estimator.
